# Case studies

Shipped games show where the trade-offs above land in practice. Two of them, The Last of Us and Horizon Zero Dawn, don't drive their enemies with a behavior tree at all, and Hitman uses one only for the characters gameplay depends on.
Halo 2 / Halo 3 (Bungie)

Isla's GDC 2005 talk[1] describes the Covenant AI as a behavior DAG (a directed acyclic graph rather than a strict tree, because one behavior subtree can occupy several places). Behaviors are self-contained modules picked by decision schemes such as prioritized lists, and extra machinery keeps the structure tractable: impulses that jump straight to a behavior, stimulus behaviors that event handlers insert at runtime, and behavior tagging that skips irrelevant relevancy checks. For Halo 3, Isla's GDC 2008 talk applies the prioritized-list idea one level up: encounter objectives form a tree of prioritized tasks that squads are poured into and filter down through, pulled up when a higher-priority task activates[24].
Unreal Engine BT system

Epic describes Unreal's BTs as "event-driven to avoid doing unnecessary work every frame": instead of polling, the tree listens for events, and conditional decorators observe blackboard keys and abort when their values change[6]. The editor's debugger highlights the active branch while the game runs, which makes it quick to see why an NPC chose a behavior. In place of a general Parallel composite, Unreal offers Simple Parallel, which runs a single main task alongside a background subtree; its Finish Mode decides whether the node ends as soon as the main task does, aborting the background, or waits for the background to finish[22].
The Last of Us (Naughty Dog)

McIntosh's account of the human enemies[15] describes an architecture built on FSMs rather than behavior trees. An NPC's top-level states, called skills (panic, advance, melee, gun combat, hide, investigate, flank and others), are prioritized: every frame each skill reports whether it wants to run, and the highest-priority one wins. Each skill runs its own state machine and pushes simpler behaviors, such as MoveToLocation, StandAndShoot and TakeCover, onto a behavior stack. Coordination goes through a global Combat Coordinator that hands out roles such as Flanker and OpportunisticShooter, so one NPC keeps shooting while the others take cover or flank. The per-frame skill check does the job a root selector does in a BT, with no tree.
Horizon Zero Dawn (Guerrilla Games)

The machines are not driven by behavior trees either. Guerrilla's own summary of their AI is "a combination of HTN planning and utility based decision making" plus "systems we developed to coordinate groups of agents"[16], and the HTN planner grew out of the one Guerrilla had used since Killzone 2. Julian Berteling's GDC 2018 talk covers the move from Killzone's human enemies in corridors to more than 25 distinct character types in an open world, including navigation for agents of very different sizes. Thompson's write-up of the talks adds two details. Which machine gets to attack is balanced by a utility function over its state, its distance to the player, whether the player knows it is there and the damage it has taken. And objects that can trip a machine's sensors (the player, rocks, fired arrows, other machines) carry information packets that tell the machine what it sensed, so different classes (Watchers for reconnaissance, Grazers for acquisition, Thunderjaws for combat) can react differently to the same event.
Hitman (IO Interactive)

Hitman is usually cited for scale, and the crowd numbers come from Kasper Fauerby's GDC Europe 2012 talk on Absolution: around 1,200 crowd agents with 500 on screen, at roughly 2 ms of PS3 PPU time for crowd AI and steering[12]. The crowd layer is not a behavior tree. Fauerby describes a state machine (idle, pending walk and walk for steering, plus gameplay states such as alert, prone and scared), and when a core mechanic such as close combat or a human shield needs a full character, a possession system upgrades the agent on demand to full NPC AI drawn from a small pool of invisible NPCs. The NPC layer is where the behavior trees are: Thompson's analysis of the 2016 game reports that its core AI "is reliant on behaviour trees", first devised for Absolution, with "over 300 active behaviour-tree-driven AI in a given level" and distant ones updated less often[13]. The split is the lesson: BTs for the characters gameplay depends on, a far cheaper model for the crowd around them.
